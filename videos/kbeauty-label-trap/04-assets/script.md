| s01 | Misconception | Here is the uncomfortable truth: an ingredient list is not a scorecard. It is a passport. It tells you who entered the formula — not how well they work together. Once you know what a label can — and cannot — prove, you stop chasing hero ingredients and start asking better questions. Five questions, in fact. At the end, we will put both bottles through the test. | 26.793s |
| s02 | Mechanism · Q1 | Question one: how much is actually there? On cosmetics sold in the United States or European Union, ingredients above one percent are generally listed from greatest to least. But once you reach the one-percent-or-less zone, the order can become flexible. So the bottom of the list is not a precise concentration chart. A star ingredient near the end is not automatically useless; some ingredients work at low levels. And one near the top is not automatically effective. The label gives you rank — not dose. Unless a brand publishes the percentage or meaningful finished-product testing, do not invent a number. | 38.281s |
| s03 | Mechanism · Q2 | Question two: which version of the ingredient? "Centella asiatica extract" can appear as one neat label name. But that name may not tell you the plant part, extraction process, or whether important compounds were standardized. A study can test one specific extract while your bottle contains another. Same passport name. Different traveler. So "contains cica" is a useful beginning — but it is not a conclusion. | 28.066s |
| s04 | Mechanism · Q3 | Question three: what is carrying it? An ingredient does not jump from bottle to skin by itself. It travels inside a vehicle: a water phase, gel, cream, or emulsion. The pH, solvents, emulsifiers, stability, and packaging can influence what remains intact, what reaches the skin, and what feels tolerable. That supporting cast is not empty filler. Humectants, emollients, occlusives, preservatives, and fragrance help make the finished formula what it is. Two products can feature the same hero ingredient — and behave very differently. | 33.608s |
| s05 | Proof · Q4 | Question four: what evidence actually matches the claim? A cell study can reveal an interesting mechanism. A human study of an ingredient can suggest potential. But neither automatically proves that this finished serum — at this concentration, in this formula — will create the same result on your skin. The closer the evidence gets to the actual product, actual people, and the outcome being promised, the more directly useful it becomes. Ask: was the finished product tested? Compared with what? On how many people? For how long? Was the change measured — or only noticed? Evidence is not a yes-or-no badge. It has distance. | 39.383s |
| s06 | Application · Q5 | Question five: where does the answer stop applying? **"Gentle" isn't a fixed property.** [K-3 reword — see below] Your current barrier, known allergies, the rest of your routine, and how often you apply a product all matter. A trustworthy answer names its boundary: who should patch-test, what combination deserves caution, when to stop, and when a dermatologist — not YouTube — is the right next step. A caveat is not weakness. It is part of the answer. | 24.642s |
| s07 | Recap · Reveal | Now, the bottles. Bottle A shouts "eighty percent cica water," but gives no useful standardization, no finished-formula evidence, and includes fragrance that our hypothetical reactive-skin shopper already avoids. Bottle B makes a quieter claim, explains the formula, publishes a relevant human test, and names its limits. Is Bottle B automatically perfect? No. But it has earned better questions — and supplied better answers. | 24.975s |
| s08 | Recap · Landing | So when a bottle promises glass skin, do not ask only, "Is cica good?" Ask: how much, which version, what vehicle, what evidence, and where is the boundary? That is how you turn an ingredient list into a choice you can defend. SeoulHabit. Korean skincare, answered. | 20.245s |
